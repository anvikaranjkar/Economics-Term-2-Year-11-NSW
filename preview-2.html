Topic 3 · Markets
Demand
Demand (definition) The quantity of a particular good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various price levels at a given point in time.

A distinction is made between individual demand (one consumer's demand) and market demand — the demand by all consumers. Market demand = sum of all individual quantities demanded at each price level.

6.1 Factors Affecting Market Demand

The main factors that influence consumer demand (other than price) are:

1. The price of other goods and services
  • Substitutes — goods that can replace each other (e.g. butter and margarine, Netflix and Disney+). If the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases.
  • Complements — goods consumed together (e.g. cars and petrol, phones and data plans). If the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good falls.
2. Expected future prices

If consumers expect prices to rise in the future (e.g. introduction of a new tax), they buy more now — increasing current demand.

3. Changes in consumer tastes and preferences

Technological progress can make older goods obsolete (e.g. CDs replaced by streaming). Changing fashion reduces demand for unfashionable goods.

4. The level of income
  • Normal goods — demand rises as income rises (e.g. luxury cars, dining out).
  • Inferior goods — demand falls as income rises (e.g. generic brand products).
  • Income distribution — redistributing income towards higher earners raises demand for luxury goods.
5. The size of the population and age distribution

Larger population → greater total demand. Ageing population (like Australia's) shifts demand towards healthcare, retirement villages, and aged care.

Network Externalities Bandwagon effect (positive): Demand rises because everyone else has the good (e.g. mobile phones).
Snob effect (negative): Demand falls as more people own the good (e.g. rare artworks, limited-edition cars).
6.2 Movements Along the Demand Curve

A change in the price of the good itself causes a movement along the demand curve (not a shift). This is called:

  • Expansion (extension) of demand — price falls → quantity demanded rises (move down-right along curve)
  • Contraction of demand — price rises → quantity demanded falls (move up-left along curve)

The law of demand states: as price increases, quantity demanded decreases (inverse relationship), assuming all other factors are constant (ceteris paribus).

Remember: movement ALONG the curve = price change only. A SHIFT of the curve = any other factor (income, tastes, etc.).

6.4 Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)
Price Elasticity of Demand Measures the responsiveness (sensitivity) of quantity demanded to a change in price. Calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
Price Elasticity of Demand PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
TypePED valueDescriptionExample
Relatively elasticPED > 1Strong response — % Δ qty > % Δ priceLuxury cars, overseas holidays
Relatively inelasticPED < 1Weak response — % Δ qty < % Δ pricePetrol, cigarettes, insulin

Perfectly elasticPED = ∞Horizontal demand curve — any price rise kills demand entirelyIndividual seller in pure competition
Perfectly inelasticPED = 0Vertical demand curve — quantity doesn't change regardless of priceLife-saving medication (theoretical)
Total Outlay Method

A simple way to determine elasticity by observing what happens to total revenue (price × quantity) when price changes:

Total Outlay Rule Price ↑ and Revenue ↑ → Inelastic demand
Price ↑ and Revenue ↓ → Elastic demand
Price ↑ and Revenue unchanged → Unit elastic
Why PED Matters
  • Business firms: Use PED to set optimal pricing. Elastic demand → lower price raises revenue. Inelastic demand → higher price raises revenue.
  • Government: Imposes excise taxes on inelastic goods (tobacco, alcohol, petrol) to raise reliable revenue with little drop in quantity purchased. Also uses PED to predict effects of indirect taxes.

Topic 3 · Markets
Supply
Supply (definition) The quantity of a good or service that all firms in an industry are willing and able to offer for sale at different price levels, at a given point in time. Market supply = sum of all individual firm supplies.
7.1 Factors Affecting Market Supply
1. The price of the good itself

Higher price → more profitable to produce → supply increases. This drives the upward-sloping supply curve (law of supply).

2. The price of other goods or services

If a substitute in production becomes more profitable (e.g. yoghurt price rises), producers shift resources away from producing cheese → cheese supply falls.

3. Expected future prices

If producers expect a future price rise, they may withhold supply now to sell at higher prices later (works mainly for storable goods).

4. The state of technology

Better technology lowers production costs and allows more to be produced at any given price → supply increases. Example: 3D printing has expanded supply of hearing aids.

5. Changes in the cost of factors of production

A fall in factor costs (wages, raw materials, energy) reduces production costs → supply increases. A rise in factor costs → supply decreases.

6. The quantity of the good available / number of suppliers

More suppliers in the industry → greater market supply. Natural limits (e.g. iridium reserves) constrain supply of rare goods.

7. Climatic and seasonal influences

Drought, floods, or extreme weather reduce agricultural supply. Seasonal conditions affect when and how much can be produced.

8. Government regulation

Tougher safety regulations or licencing requirements can restrict supply by raising compliance costs.

7.2 Movements Along the Supply Curve

A change in the price of the good itself causes movement along the supply curve:

  • Expansion of supply — price rises → quantity supplied rises (move up-right)
  • Contraction of supply — price falls → quantity supplied falls (move down-left)

The law of supply: as price increases, quantity supplied increases (positive relationship), ceteris paribus.

Topic 3 · Markets
Market Equilibrium
8.1 The Concept of Market Equilibrium
Market Equilibrium The situation where, at a certain price level, the quantity supplied and the quantity demanded of a particular good are equal. The market clears — there is no excess demand or excess supply — and there is no tendency for price to change.
Price Mechanism The process by which the forces of supply and demand interact to determine the market price at which goods and services will be bought and sold, and the quantity produced.

The analysis assumes pure competition — many buyers and sellers, no single participant has the power to influence market outcomes.

8.2 Establishing Market Equilibrium

Equilibrium is found at the intersection of the demand (D) and supply (S) curves, giving the equilibrium price (PE) and equilibrium quantity (QE).

Adjustment Mechanism If price > equilibrium (excess supply): Quantity supplied > quantity demanded. Sellers compete to sell → price falls → expansion of demand + contraction of supply → market returns to equilibrium.

If price < equilibrium (excess demand): Quantity demanded > quantity supplied. Buyers compete to buy → price rises → contraction of demand + expansion of supply → market returns to equilibrium.
Market Equilibrium Occurs When: 1. Quantity demanded = quantity supplied
2. The market clears (no surplus or shortage)
3. There is no tendency for price or quantity to change

8.4 The Role of the Market

The price mechanism solves the economic problem (what, how, and for whom to produce) through:

  • Product markets — goods and services. D curve = consumer wants; S curve = production limits. Equilibrium price satisfies both.
  • Factor markets — inputs (land, labour, capital, enterprise). D and S of factor inputs determines the price (wage, rent, profit) received by resource owners.
Allocative Efficiency The economy's ability to allocate resources to satisfy consumer wants. Achieved when production continues until the value consumers place on the last unit equals the cost of producing it. Price mechanism ensures allocative efficiency by sending signals to producers and consumers.

Competition ensures firms are responsive to consumer demand and produce at minimum cost (productive efficiency).

8.5 Government Intervention in the Marketplace
Market Failure Occurs when the price mechanism fails to take into account all the social costs and benefits of production and consumption, resulting in a socially suboptimal outcome (too much or too little produced).
Price Intervention
  • Price ceiling (maximum price) — set below equilibrium price. Prevents price rising above this level. Creates excess demand (shortage). Example: government caps on public transport fares or rent controls.
  • Price floor (minimum price) — set above equilibrium price. Prevents price falling below this level. Creates excess supply (surplus). Example: minimum wage in the labour market.
⚠ Both price ceilings and floors cause market disequilibrium Price ceiling → shortage (QD > QS)
Price floor → surplus (QS > QD)
Quantity Intervention — Externalities
Externality A cost or benefit that affects parties other than the buyer and seller involved in a transaction — a side-effect not captured by the price mechanism.
  • Negative externality — a cost borne by society (e.g. pollution from industry). Social cost > private cost → market overproduces. Government response: tax (e.g. carbon tax), regulations, quantity limits.
  • Positive externality — a benefit enjoyed by society (e.g. education, public parks). Social benefit > private benefit → market underproduces. Government response: subsidies to producers or consumers.
ProblemGovernment ActionOutcome

Merit Goods Goods not produced in sufficient quantity by private markets because individuals do not fully value the external benefits (e.g. healthcare, education). Government subsidises these.
Public Goods Goods that private firms are unwilling to supply because they are non-excludable and non-rival in consumption (benefits can't be restricted to paying consumers). Example: national defence, public roads, street lighting. Government must provide these.
8.6 Competition and Market Power
Market StructureKey CharacteristicsPrice Setting
Pure competitionMany buyers & sellers; homogeneous product; no barriers to entry; no market power; theoreticalPrice taker — accepts market price
Monopolistic competitionMany small firms; differentiated products; some barriers to entry; brand loyaltySome price-setting power via differentiation
OligopolyFew large dominant firms; significant barriers to entry; interdependent pricingSignificant price-setting power
MonopolyOne seller; no substitutes; high barriers; no competitionPrice setter — controls market price

As competition decreases, prices tend to rise and output falls relative to pure competition. Greater market power → higher equilibrium price and lower equilibrium quantity than under pure competition.

Monopoly Characteristics Only one firm selling the product. No close substitutes. Significant barriers to entry. The monopolist is a price setter and can restrict output to maximise profit. In Australia, water supply is an example of a natural monopoly.

Topic 4 · Labour Markets
Labour Demand and Supply
Labour Market Where individuals seeking employment interact with employers who want to obtain the most appropriate labour skills for the production process. The price of labour is the wage/salary.

In reality, there are many distinct labour markets (by occupation, industry, region). For simplicity, economists refer to "the labour market" as if it were one.

9.1 The Demand for Labour
Derived Demand Labour demand is a derived demand — firms demand labour not for its own sake, but because it is needed to produce goods and services that consumers want. If demand for output rises, demand for labour rises.

Like other demand curves, the labour demand curve slopes downward — as wages fall, firms employ more labour.

Factors Affecting Labour Demand
Output factors (affecting how much a firm produces)
  • The output of the firm / general economic conditions (aggregate demand): The single most important factor. Strong economic growth → higher sales → firms hire more workers. Recessions cause firms to reduce staff.
  • The demand for an individual firm's products: Even in a declining industry, a firm increasing its market share will demand more labour. Influenced by product quality, marketing, reputation.
  • Conditions in the firm's industry: Changes in consumer preferences, industry regulation, and barriers to entry affect whole-industry labour demand. E.g. Australia's ageing population has boosted demand for aged care and health workers.
Output factors (affecting how labour is used to produce)
  • The productivity of labour: Labour productivity = output per unit of labour per unit of time. Higher productivity means fewer workers needed for the same output (short-run effect). But if aggregate demand is rising faster than productivity gains, demand for labour still rises.
  • The cost of labour versus other inputs (capital): Firms compare the cost of labour with capital (machinery). If wages rise relative to capital costs (interest rates), firms substitute capital for labour → labour demand falls. Labour demand is more elastic when:
    • It is easy to substitute between labour and capital
    • Labour costs are a large proportion of total costs
    • It is easy to pass on higher costs to consumers
  • Cost of foreign labour: If offshore labour is cheaper (e.g. manufacturers moving to China or India), domestic labour demand falls. Example: Australian call centres shifting to the Philippines.
Summary: Factors Influencing Labour Demand Output factors: General economic conditions · Firm's individual product demand · Industry conditions
Input use factors: Productivity of labour · Cost of labour vs capital · Cost of foreign labour
Labour Productivity
Labour Productivity Labour Productivity = Total Output ÷ Labour Input

Labour productivity depends on: education and skills, health and motivation, technology, and how efficiently labour is combined with other inputs.

9.2 The Supply of Labour

Labour supply slopes upward — as wages rise, more individuals are willing to supply labour. Unlike product markets, individuals (workers) supply labour; firms demand it.

Factors Affecting Labour Supply
1. Pay levels (wages)

Higher wage → more people willing to sacrifice leisure to work. Non-wage incentives (bonuses, superannuation, flexible hours) also influence supply to a particular firm or industry.

2. Working conditions

Attractive conditions (flexibility, remote work options, pleasant environment) attract more workers. Since 2024, Australian workers have a legal "right to disconnect" from work outside hours. 36% of Australians regularly worked from home in 2024 post-COVID.

3. Education, skills and experience requirements (Human Capital)
Human Capital The total sum of knowledge, skills, training, and experience of workers that contributes to the production process. It is the "quality" of the labour force and the main influence on productivity growth.

Labour supply is limited by the availability of human capital. Jobs requiring advanced skills (e.g. medical specialists) have inelastic supply — takes years to train.

4. The mobility of labour
  • Occupational mobility — ability to move between different occupations. Higher for low-skill jobs; lower for highly specialised professions.
  • Geographical mobility — ability to move between locations. Limited by relocation costs, family ties, regional opportunities. Remote working has reduced geographic barriers significantly.
5. Immigration policies

Australia's immigration system prioritises skilled workers (70%+ of adult immigrants enter on skills basis). Immigration expands labour supply. Over 1 million net overseas arrivals in the 2 years to March 2024 (an all-time high). As of 2025, cuts of 140,000/year planned.

6. Other factors
  • Professional associations (e.g. Law Society, AMA) set standards that restrict labour supply to certain occupations.
  • Government licencing and qualification requirements can limit supply (e.g. builder's licence).
  • Participation rate — proportion of working-age population in the workforce.
9.3 The Australian Workforce — Key Definitions & Calculations
⭐ This section is calculation-heavy — must know all 4 formulas Unemployment rate, labour force, participation rate, and working-age population calculations are all assessed.
Workforce / Labour Force The number of people 15 years of age and above who are either working or actively seeking work. Includes: employed persons + unemployed persons.
Working-Age Population The number of people in an economy who are at least age 15 (the working age). Technically defined as people between 15 and 64 in some contexts, but in Australia the ABS includes all aged 15 and over as potential workforce participants.
Labour Force (Workforce) Labour Force = Employed + Unemployed
Employed A person is employed if they work at least one hour per week. Includes: full-time workers, part-time workers, those on paid leave, strike, or workers' compensation.
Unemployed A person is unemployed if they are: (1) currently available for work, AND (2) actively seeking work, but have not yet found it. Must satisfy BOTH conditions.
Not in the Labour Force People of working age who are neither employed nor actively seeking work. Includes: retired people, full-time students, people performing domestic duties, discouraged workers (those who have given up looking).
Unemployment Rate Unemployment Rate (%) = (Number Unemployed ÷ Labour Force) × 100
Labour Force Participation Rate Participation Rate (%) = (Labour Force ÷ Working-Age Population [15+]) × 100
Discouraged workers are NOT counted as unemployed — they're "not in the labour force." This means the official unemployment rate can understate the true extent of unemployment.
Population Size and Growth

Australia's population is approx. 26.5 million (2024). Population growth comes from two sources:

  • Natural increase — excess of births over deaths in a year. Rate has declined (below 0.6% in early 2020s) due to fewer children per family.
  • Net migration — excess of permanent arrivals over departures. Accounts for ~40% of Australia's population growth. Record net migration of 1 million in 2 years post-COVID.
Age Distribution

Australia's population is ageing — proportion aged 65+ is growing, while 15–64 share is declining. Median age rose from 32 (1991) to 38 (2024); projected to reach 43.1 by 2062–63. This creates long-term pressure on the labour force size and aged care demand.

Key trends: male participation has declined significantly. Female participation has risen dramatically — from 39% in 1969 to a record 63.2% in 2025. Overall rate is at a record high of 67%.

Factors affecting participation rate:
  • State of the economy (pro-cyclical): Boom → higher participation (more jobs available). Recession → lower participation (discouraged workers give up).
  • Ageing population: Long-term decline expected as older cohorts retire. Government has raised pension age to 67 (2023) to counteract this.
  • Female participation: Rising due to changing social attitudes, childcare availability, and lower fertility rates.
  • School retention rates: More young people staying in education reduces short-run participation rate.
  • Immigration: Skills-based immigration increases participation rate.
Quality of the Workforce — Education
  • Higher education = higher earnings. Postgraduate degree holders earn ~2× as much as Year 12 only.
  • Across OECD (2023), unemployment among those without secondary completion was ~6 percentage points higher than tertiary completers.
  • 86.2% of 20–24 year olds had completed Year 12 in 2024.
  • Australia's education spending is slightly above OECD average; ~1/3 of education expenditure is privately funded (one of the largest shares in OECD).
9.4 Extension: Labour Market Equilibrium
Note: Extension Content This section goes beyond the syllabus minimum but helps understand how the labour market fits with other markets. It is useful context for extended responses.

Applying standard supply and demand to the labour market:

  • Supply curve slopes upward — higher wages attract more workers willing to supply labour. Note: labour supply can be backward-bending at very high wages (workers choose leisure over more income).
  • Demand curve slopes downward — at lower wages, firms employ more workers (law of diminishing marginal returns).
  • Equilibrium — achieved at the intersection of labour supply and demand. WE = equilibrium wage; QE = equilibrium employment level.
Changes in Labour Market Equilibrium Increase in labour demand (D shifts right): → wage rises, employment rises
Decrease in labour demand (D shifts left): → wage falls, employment falls
People choose more leisure (S shifts left): → wage rises, employment falls
Immigration increases supply (S shifts right): → wage may fall, employment rises

In reality, the labour market does NOT function as a perfectly competitive market — institutions (trade unions, minimum wages, awards) mean the wage rate is not solely determined by free market forces.
