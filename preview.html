Section 1 Multiple Choice 5 questions · 5 marks
Question 1 1 mark
Which of the following would cause a decrease in the demand for a good — that is, a leftward shift of the demand curve?
A A fall in the price of a complementary good
B An increase in consumer income for a normal good
C A rise in the price of a substitute good
D A technological improvement making the good obsolete

Correct Answer: D

A technological improvement that makes a good obsolete (e.g. CDs when streaming emerged) reduces consumer desire for it — a non-price factor that shifts demand left. Option A (cheaper complement) would increase demand. Option B (higher income for normal good) increases demand. Option C (pricier substitute) also increases demand as consumers switch.
Question 2 1 mark
In a competitive market, the price of petrol rises sharply. What is the most likely short-run outcome for the price elasticity of supply of petrol?
A Relatively elastic, because producers have time to build new refineries
B Relatively inelastic, because producers cannot quickly increase their inputs
C Perfectly elastic, because oil is available globally
D Unit elastic, because the price increase exactly matches the supply increase

Correct Answer: B

In the short run, supply is relatively inelastic because producers have fixed inputs and cannot rapidly expand capacity (e.g. building new refineries takes years). In the long run, supply becomes more elastic as firms can adjust all inputs.
Question 3 1 mark
Market equilibrium is best described as the point where:
A The government sets a fair price for all consumers
B Quantity demanded equals quantity supplied and there is no tendency for price to change
C Producers maximise their profit regardless of consumer demand
D The market clears only if there is excess supply

Correct Answer: B

Market equilibrium occurs when quantity demanded = quantity supplied at the prevailing price, meaning the market clears and there is no excess demand or excess supply — hence no pressure for price to change.
Question 4 1 mark
A worker who is currently not employed and has stopped looking for work because they believe no jobs are available would be classified as:
A Unemployed
B Employed part-time
C Not in the labour force
D Structurally unemployed

Correct Answer: C

To be counted as unemployed, a person must be available for work AND actively seeking it. A person who has given up searching is called a discouraged worker and is classified as "not in the labour force" — they are excluded from both the employed and unemployed categories, which can cause the official unemployment rate to understate true unemployment.
Question 5 1 mark
The demand for labour is described as a "derived demand" because:
A Workers determine their own wages through collective bargaining
B Employers demand labour only because it is needed to produce goods and services that consumers want
C Labour supply always exceeds demand in a market economy
D The government derives labour requirements from national productivity targets

Correct Answer: B

Labour demand is derived from the demand for the goods and services that workers help to produce. Firms only hire labour because it generates output that consumers wish to buy — so labour demand ultimately reflects consumer demand in product markets.
Section 2 Short Answer 15 marks
Question 6 4 marks
(a) Define price elasticity of demand and state the formula used to calculate it. (2 marks)

Model Answer

Definition: Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures the responsiveness (or sensitivity) of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its price.

Formula:
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

If PED > 1 → relatively elastic demand (strong response).
If PED < 1 → relatively inelastic demand (weak response).
If PED = 1 → unit elastic demand.
(b) — 2 marks
Using a real-world example, explain why the government tends to impose excise taxes on goods with relatively inelastic demand (such as tobacco and alcohol).

Model Answer

When demand is relatively inelastic, consumers do not reduce their quantity demanded much in response to a price increase. Therefore, when the government adds an excise tax to goods like cigarettes, the tax-induced price rise causes only a small drop in quantity sold. This means the government collects a large and predictable amount of tax revenue. For example, even if the price of a packet of cigarettes rises due to an excise tax, addicted smokers continue to purchase roughly the same quantity, so government revenue is maximised. By contrast, taxing a good with elastic demand would sharply reduce sales and yield little revenue.

Question 8 6 marks
Outline three factors that could cause the demand for labour to increase in a particular industry. For each factor, explain the mechanism by which it leads to higher labour demand. (2 marks each)
Hint: Think about what drives firms to hire more workers — output, productivity, conditions in the firm's industry…

Model Answer (any 3 well-explained factors)

  • 1. Increase in aggregate demand / general economic conditions: When the economy grows and consumer spending rises, firms sell more goods and services. To produce this higher output, firms need to employ more workers, so labour demand increases. For example, a boom in consumer spending increases retail output, leading supermarkets to hire more staff.
  • 2. Rise in demand for an individual firm's products: If a specific firm's product becomes more popular — due to successful marketing, improved quality, or a competitor failing — the firm must increase output and therefore hire additional labour. E.g. a surge in demand for electric vehicles increases demand for workers in EV manufacturing.
  • 3. Higher labour productivity (accompanied by rising aggregate demand): When workers become more productive, each worker generates more output. If aggregate demand is also rising, firms will demand more labour to meet higher sales. However, note that if demand is unchanged, higher productivity may actually reduce labour demand (fewer workers needed for the same output).
  • 4. Fall in the cost of other inputs / cost of labour relative to capital rises: If the cost of capital (machinery) rises relative to labour (e.g. higher interest rates making borrowing for equipment expensive), firms substitute labour for capital, increasing labour demand.
  • 5. Conditions in the firm's industry: Changes in consumer preferences, lower barriers to entry, or reduced regulation that boost an industry's output will increase the demand for workers in that industry. E.g. the rapid growth of aged care services due to Australia's ageing population has significantly increased labour demand for care workers.

Model Answer Outline (Band 13–15 Response)

Introduction: Briefly define markets, supply, and demand as the two forces that interact via the price mechanism to determine price and quantity.

Supply & Demand:
  • Demand — downward sloping; factors affecting it (price, income, substitutes, complements, tastes, population, expectations). Distinguish between movement along vs shift of curve.
  • Supply — upward sloping; factors affecting it (cost of production, technology, government policy, number of producers, prices of other goods). Distinguish movement vs shift.
  • Market equilibrium — intersection of D and S. Diagram showing Pe and Qe. Explain adjustment mechanism: if price above equilibrium → excess supply → price falls; if below → excess demand → price rises.
Price Mechanism & Allocative Efficiency:
  • Price mechanism = the process by which price signals coordinate decisions of buyers and sellers without central planning.
  • Allocative efficiency — resources allocated where they are most valued; production continues until the value to the last consumer equals the cost to the last producer.
  • Example: Rising demand for EVs → price rises → profit signal attracts more producers → supply increases → more EVs produced. Price conveys information efficiently.
Market Failure:
  • Define market failure: where the price mechanism fails to account for all social costs and benefits, leading to a socially suboptimal quantity being produced/consumed.
  • Example — Negative externality (e.g. pollution from coal-fired power): Social cost > private cost → market overproduces → diagram showing social supply curve above private supply curve, socially optimal Qs < market Qm.
  • Government response: carbon tax, regulation, etc. to correct the externality.
Conclusion: The price mechanism is a powerful but imperfect tool. While it achieves allocative efficiency under ideal conditions, market failures — such as externalities — demonstrate that government intervention is sometimes necessary to improve social outcomes.
